Theme 01 · Monetary Policy

Banxico & Monetary Policy

Over these six months Banxico did three things in sequence: paused, delivered two more cuts, then formally closed the easing cycle and went on hold. The rate path itself was almost boring — 7.00% → 6.75% → 6.50%, ending exactly where both desks had pencilled in back in December. What was not boring was the road there.

A genuine dovish detour in February (when JPM briefly called a 6.25% terminal via three back-to-back cuts), a hawkish reversal after a split March decision, and a late, externally-triggered convergence when the Governor pre-announced the final cut. By late June the rate sat at 6.50%, both houses saw it parked there, and the live debate had inverted — the market was pricing a year-end hike that neither desk believed in.

Phase 0 — Starting point: the December cut and the signalled pause

Banxico cut 25bp to 7.00% at its final 2025 meeting on 18 December — the lowest since June 2022 — on a non-consensus 4-1 vote, and signalled a pause: "the Board will evaluate the timing for additional reference rate adjustments" [Citi C023, 18 Dec 2025, p.2]. The real ex-ante rate was 3.1% and the Banxico–Fed differential 325bp [JPM J001, 19 Dec 2025, p.1].

Both desks framed the pause identically but set up different 2026 paths. Citi read it as time to assess January's tariff/excise-tax pass-through (11–25bp from tariffs, ~20bp from excise) and raised its end-2026 call to 6.50% (from 6.25%), with cuts resuming in May and June [Citi C023, 18 Dec 2025]. JPM was more hawkish on quantity, seeing "only one more cut to 6.75%" [JPM J001, 19 Dec 2025, p.1]. Both ended the year near 6.50% — but via different routes.

Phase 1 — Wait-and-see, then a dovish pivot (Jan – 5 Feb)

The December minutes (8 Jan) confirmed the pause: most members cited slack, a strong peso and anchored expectations, but wanted to see the tax pass-through first; the lone hawk called Banxico's inflation path "non-credible" [JPM J005, 8 Jan 2026, p.1]. Then the data turned benign and the tone shifted fast:

  • The 2026 Monetary Program (~27 Jan) framed tax/tariff effects as limited and transitory, the Board "not done yet" [JPM J012, 28 Jan 2026].
  • JPM pivoted hard. On 28 Jan it flagged a "meaningful risk of resuming cuts as soon as March" and put 25% odds on a 6.00% terminal this year [JPM J012, 28 Jan 2026, p.2]. A week later it made the dovish case its base: three back-to-back cuts (Mar/May/Jun) to a 6.25% terminal — its most aggressive call of the period, and below Citi [JPM J016, 5 Feb 2026, p.1].
  • Citi moved the same way but less far, carrying a "bias toward front-loading to March" without yet changing its two-cut, 6.50% base case [Citi C014, 11 Feb 2026, p.3].

The 5 February decision: unanimous hold at 7.00%, with the door reopened to a cut "as soon as" the next meeting. Banxico staff saw inflation peaking ~4% in 1Q, easing to 3.5% by year-end [JPM J016, 5 Feb 2026, p.1].

Divergence · early February

JPM = three cuts to 6.25%; Citi = two cuts to 6.50%. Counter to the usual stereotype, JPM was the dove. This gap drove the next two months.

Phase 2 — The March cut and the terminal-rate reversal (13 Feb – 9 Apr)

The February minutes (19 Feb) hardened the "March is live" view: the pause "could be shorter than originally expected" — a line JPM attributed to Governor Victoria Rodríguez [JPM J021, 19 Feb 2026, p.1]. Citi formally changed its call the same day: "We recently changed our monetary policy call by front-loading the rate cuts to March… reaching a level of 6.50%" [Citi C007, 19 Feb 2026, p.1], and pulled USDMXN to 18.00 (from 18.87). The 26 Feb Quarterly Inflation Report added a dovish growth tilt — 2026 GDP raised to 1.6% (from 1.1%) [Citi C017, 27 Feb 2026].

But a March geopolitical oil shock eroded JPM's aggressive call. By 20 March it warned: "what is at risk is our call for a terminal rate of 6.25%, not next week's decision" [JPM J031, 20 Mar 2026, p.1], lifting year-end inflation to 4.3% on ~US$90/bbl oil. Citi, by contrast, held its 6.50% terminal and treated 26 March as a "close call" it expected to resolve in a cut [Citi C003, 18 Mar 2026].

The 26 March decision: cut 25bp to 6.75% on a 3-2 split (deputies Borja and Heath dissenting) [JPM J034, 26 Mar 2026, p.1]. Both desks read the split plus more conditional guidance as more data-dependent / hawkish-leaning than the cut implied [Citi C012, 27 Mar 2026]. Their reactions diverged then re-converged:

  • JPM capitulated on its dovish call — abandoned 6.25%, moved to "a final cut to 6.50% in June after a pause in May," converging up to Citi [JPM J034, 26 Mar 2026, p.1].
  • Citi pushed its final cut out — "delaying the last 25-bps cut… to at least June (previously May)" [Citi C012, 27 Mar 2026].
Note for the cross-theme read

By late March JPM's growth call was deteriorating fast (2026 GDP cut 1.5% → 1.3%, "second downward revision in two weeks"), feeding directly into the rate debate. Picked up in the Growth chapter.

Phase 3 — The final cut: an externally-triggered convergence (28 Apr – 7 May)

The cleanest case in the whole corpus of one official communication moving both desks at once. On 28 April, Governor Rodríguez told the Senate she expected the Board to "consider a final cut to our benchmark rate in our next decision" and that "we are near the end of the easing cycle we began in May 2024" [Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026, p.2]. Within 48 hours both houses moved their final cut forward from June to 7 May: JPM [JPM J050, 30 Apr 2026] and Citi ("We changed our call to a 25-bps rate cut in the May 7 meeting from a pause") [Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026, p.1]. Both also absorbed a grim flash 1Q26 GDP of −3.0% saar.

The 7 May decision: cut 25bp to 6.50% on another 3-2 split, with an explicit close: "…maintain the reference rate at its current level… thereby conclude the cycle that began in March 2024" [Citi C010, 8 May 2026, p.1]. JPM judged the statement "slightly hawkish" and set its forward call: "on hold for the foreseeable future at 6.5% after the longest easing cycle on record (475bps)" [JPM J057, 7 May 2026, p.2].

Phase 4 — On hold, and the "neutral narrative" (8 May – 26 Jun)

With the cycle closed, the debate flipped from when's the next cut to how long is the hold — and could the next move be a hike. Three threads ran through it:

1. The Board entrenched the pause. The May minutes (21 May) had all members — including dissenters — agreeing 6.50% is the terminal rate, no signal in either direction; Heath the most hawkish on core-services stickiness [JPM J068, 21 May 2026, p.1]. The 27 May QIR cut Banxico's own 2026 growth forecast to 1.1% (from 1.6%) while leaving inflation broadly unchanged [JPM J076, 27 May 2026]; JPM extended its on-hold horizon to end-2027. The 25 June decision was a unanimous hold at 6.50%, guidance reaffirmed; Banxico also introduced a new, fully-sterilised liquidity tool (auction purchases of government securities) that JPM stressed is not QE [JPM J092, 25 Jun 2026, p.2].

2. Fiscal/ratings noise pressed in (detailed in the Fiscal chapter). S&P moved its BBB outlook to negative (12 May) and Moody's downgraded Mexico to Baa3 on 21–22 May [Citi C016, 13 May 2026; JPM J065, 22 May 2026, p.1] — JPM had pre-called the Moody's move. It sharpened the "fiscal dominance" watch but did not change the rate calls.

3. The hike the market priced and the desks rejected. By mid-June the market was pricing close to a full 25bp hike by year-end. Both pushed back: Citi — "the bar for a rate hike remains high," risk in fact "tilted more toward lower rates" [Citi C021, 18 Jun 2026, p.1]; JPM — on hold, any move dictated chiefly by the Fed [JPM J088, 18 Jun 2026]. The period closed dovish-leaning: 1H-June headline CPI fell to 3.55% oya (inside target), prompting JPM to trim year-end inflation to 4.1% and, on a strong April activity print, raise 2026 growth to 1.2% [JPM J094, 26 Jun 2026]. Citi held year-end inflation at 4.4% to absorb a newly-elevated El Niño risk [Citi C022, 25 Jun 2026].

How the two desks differed

They agreed on the destination (6.50%) and the end-of-cycle timing; the differences were in conviction, sequencing and risk-skew:

  • Who was the dove, and when. JPM was the more aggressive easer in Feb–Mar (6.25% via three cuts) while Citi never left 6.50%. The "consensus" that formed by April was really JPM abandoning a lonely dovish call.
  • Reading the statements. Citi consistently labelled the March and May statements as carrying a "hawkish bias"; JPM agreed on the wording but framed the decisions as net-dovish relative to a strict reaction function (inflation was >4.5% when they cut).
  • Post-cycle risk skew. After 7 May, Citi saw risk skewed to the downside (more cuts); JPM was more symmetric, leaning on the Fed / relative-conditions channel and sticky core-services as the swing factors.
  • Growth in the home stretch. By late June JPM's growth view recovered faster (1.2%) than Citi's, which kept trimming its GDP median to 1.1% even as activity firmed.

Theme 02 · Prices

Inflation

Headline inflation spent the half-year as a story about non-core food and energy, not underlying price pressure. It entered 2026 at 3.7%, was dragged up to a 4.59% peak in March almost entirely by tomatoes and a Middle-East oil scare, and then fell back to 3.55% by mid-June — inside Banxico's target band — as those shocks reversed. Underneath, core did something much calmer: it rose to ~4.5% on January's tax hikes, then glided steadily down to 4.1%, anchored by a strong peso. The market's January fear — that tariffs would feed through to prices — never really materialised; the recurring villain was the weather.

Phase 0 — A benign close to 2025 (Dec)

Mexico ended 2025 with inflation looking tame: headline at 3.69% oya in December (the 1H-Dec print of +0.17% was the lowest since 2019), core at 4.33% [JPM J004, 8 Jan 2026; Citi C027, 9 Jan 2026, p11]. Non-core was extraordinarily soft — raw food running −5.6% oya, non-core just 1.8% vs a ~5.9% historical mean [JPM J004, 8 Jan 2026]. The worry was all forward-looking: January would bring excise-tax hikes (sugary drinks, tobacco, processed food), new tariffs on non-FTA imports, and a 13% minimum-wage increase [Citi C023, 18 Dec 2025, p3]. JPM initially feared core could approach 5% by March; Citi sized the tax hit at ~20bp and tariffs at 11–25bp.

Phase 1 — "Taxes, not tariffs (yet)" (Jan – Feb)

The January pass-through proved milder than feared. Headline came in at 3.79% oya (0.38% m/m) and core at 4.52% [JPM J017, 13 Feb 2026]. The excise-tax effect was a one-off ~13bp (below the ~20bp expected), and crucially the tariff effect barely showed — core non-food goods ran below their historical median, which Citi titled bluntly: "Inflation: taxes, not tariffs (yet)" [Citi C013, 22 Jan 2026]. Both desks credited the strong peso for limiting goods pass-through [JPM J019, 13 Feb 2026]. JPM trimmed its year-end headline call to 3.7–3.8% [JPM J012, 28 Jan 2026]. Banxico's own February survey nudged 2026 expectations only marginally higher (~3.95–4.0%).

Phase 2 — The non-core scare: tomatoes and oil (late Feb – Mar)

Then headline tore higher — but for reasons that had little to do with the economy. February reached 4.02% oya, and 1H March hit 0.62% 2w/2w (4.63% oya), a print JPM filed under "stagflation risk unlocked" [JPM J032, 24 Mar 2026]. Two shocks:

  • Tomatoes. JPM devoted a whole note to it: the US withdrawal from the 2019 tomato suspension agreement plus a Florida winter freeze (~80% production loss) shifted US demand onto Mexican supply, creating domestic scarcity even though Mexico is a net exporter. Tomatoes alone added ~22bp to the 1H-March headline [JPM J036, 1 Apr 2026].
  • A Middle-East oil shock pushed up airfares (jet fuel), electricity (natural gas) and gasoline risk. Citi modelled a bull case (Brent to $130) lifting core toward ~5% and headline to 4.5% [Citi C012, 27 Mar 2026]; JPM raised its year-end call to 4.3% on a ~US$90/bbl Mexican mix [JPM J031, 20 Mar 2026].

Critically, core stayed contained (~4.45–4.50%) throughout the scare — cooler demand and the strong peso held core goods down. And gasoline was shielded: the government used its excise-tax mechanism to keep regular gasoline under Sheinbaum's MXN 24/litre cap, and capped diesel at MXN 27 [Citi C003, 18 Mar 2026; Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026, p2]. March monthly headline of 4.59% oya was the highest since July 2024, but JPM called the underlying message "constructive" given the raw-food driver was set to reverse [JPM J038, 10 Apr 2026].

Phase 3 — Disinflation resumes; core is the anchor (Apr – May)

The reversal came fast. Late-April delivered a big downside surprise (2H-April +0.04% 2w/2w vs a 0.24% forecast) [JPM J056, 7 May 2026], and by May headline fell to 3.94% oya — below Banxico's 4% ceiling for the first time since January [JPM J084, 9 Jun 2026]. The real signal was core: it decelerated from 4.45% (March) to 4.26% (April) to 4.19% (May), with seasonally-adjusted momentum down to ~4.1% 3m/3m saar — its lowest since December — as core goods dropped toward 4.1% on peso strength and discounting [JPM J056, 7 May 2026; JPM J069, 22 May 2026]. JPM flagged core as "an important anchor for monetary policy purposes" [JPM J043, 17 Apr 2026]. The one laggard: services, sticky near 4.5%, with "other services" (airfares/jet fuel) running above 5%.

Phase 4 — Below the ceiling, with El Niño on the horizon (Jun)

1H June sealed the disinflation: headline 3.55% oya, the lowest since October 2025, as tomato prices collapsed (−24% 2w/2w, with "room to fall a further 30%") [JPM J091, 24 Jun 2026]. Core eased to 4.12%, though SA services stayed "stubborn" at 4.74% 3m/3m saar, now also carrying World Cup pressure on airfares, hotels and travel [JPM J091, 24 Jun 2026; Citi C021, 18 Jun 2026]. On the back of the faster food reversal, JPM cut its year-end headline forecast to 4.1% (from 4.3%) — its first downgrade of the period — keeping core at 4.0% [JPM J091, 24 Jun 2026]. Banxico nudged its own 2Q path down to 4.0% while raising core slightly [Citi C022, 25 Jun 2026, p2].

Divergence · year-end 2026 headline

JPM 4.1% (cut in June on the food reversal) vs Citi 4.4% (held). The gap is almost entirely El Niño: with NOAA putting ~80% odds on El Niño by 3Q, Citi kept its forecast elevated to pre-absorb a 29–59bp cumulative hit to non-core food, while JPM treated the agri reversal as the dominant near-term force [Citi C009, 28 May 2026].

The three moving parts — and the one that crept up

  • Non-core (food + energy) = all the volatility. Tomatoes/limes and the oil scare drove the entire round-trip from 3.7% to 4.6% and back to 3.5%. Gasoline was deliberately neutralised by the excise-tax cap mechanism.
  • Core goods = the disinflation engine. The strong peso (USDMXN appreciating, investors flagging a possible break below 17.00) kept imported-goods prices falling — the single most cited reason core behaved [Citi C014, 11 Feb 2026; JPM J056, 7 May 2026].
  • Services = the stubborn laggard. Sticky at ~4.5–5%, lifted by the 13% minimum-wage hike feeding restaurants, and late in the period by World Cup tourism.
  • Expectations drifted up. Banxico's survey 2026 median rose from 3.9% (Dec) to ~4.35% (May–Jun), with long-term expectations edging to ~3.75% — what Citi called "a plateau above target" [Citi C018, 4 Jun 2026, p5]. This quiet de-anchoring risk is the thread connecting inflation to the Banxico chapter's hawkish dissents.

Theme 03 · Real economy

Growth & Activity

Mexico limped out of 2025 (full-year GDP just +0.6%) but with enough end-year momentum that both desks spent January–February raising their 2026 calls. Then the first quarter fell apart — a broad-based −2.4% saar contraction in 1Q26 — and the calls came back down. By June the question was whether the spring data marked a genuine rebound: JPM said yes (upgrading 2Q to a 5.5% saar bounce) while Citi stayed cautious, cutting consumption and investment on a delayed USMCA. Through it all, the structural picture barely moved: investment was the persistent weak spot, consumption resilient but slowing, and the output gap firmly negative — the slack that underwrote Banxico's cuts.

Phase 0 — 2025 ends weak, but with momentum (Dec – Jan)

Full-year 2025 growth landed at just +0.6% y/y, with investment down ~7% and industrial production −1.3% [JPM J018, 11 Feb 2026; JPM J022, 23 Feb 2026]. But 4Q25 rebounded +0.9% q/q (≈3.5% saar) on construction and services, leaving a healthy statistical carryover of ~0.6–0.8% into 2026 [JPM J013, 30 Jan 2026; Citi C017, 27 Feb 2026]. That carryover, plus a resilient US economy, is what drove the early-year optimism.

Phase 1 — Carryover optimism (late Jan – Feb)

Both desks marked their 2026 calls up: Citi to 1.4% (from 1.0%) and JPM all the way to 1.7% by late February, citing "green shoots" and the strong handoff [Citi C020, 30 Jan 2026; JPM J022, 23 Feb 2026]. But the leading indicators never confirmed it — manufacturing PMIs sat below 50, business confidence was contractionary, and gross fixed investment kept shrinking (machinery & equipment had been falling since 2024) [JPM J015, 6 Feb 2026; Citi C011, 5 Feb 2026]. The optimism rested on carryover and consumption, not on a genuine acceleration.

Phase 2 — The 1Q26 collapse (Mar – Apr)

The cracks opened in March. The January GDP proxy contracted −0.9% samr (versus a −0.2% expectation), and JPM cut its 2026 call from 1.7% to 1.3% in a note titled "Stagflation risk unlocked" — its "second downward revision in two weeks" [JPM J032, 24 Mar 2026]. Activity then deteriorated through April (a near-flat February, sharp retail-sales drop), pushing JPM to −2.5% then to the flash 1Q26 reading of −3.0% saar (30 Apr), with the full-year call down to 1.0% [JPM J051, 30 Apr 2026]. Citi tracked the same path down to 1.1% [Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026]. The final 1Q print was revised to −2.4% saar, and it was broad-based — services −1.5%, industry −4.0%, agriculture −6.6%, with private investment cratering (−13% annualised) and only a public-capex surge cushioning the fall [JPM J070, 22 May 2026; Citi C021, 18 Jun 2026].

Phase 3 — The rebound debate (May – Jun)

Spring data then turned up. The March IGAE rose +0.4% samr and April surprised strongly — IGAE +1.2% samr and IP +2.1% samr (construction +7.6%) [JPM J090, 23 Jun 2026; JPM J086, 11 Jun 2026]. Here the desks split:

  • JPM turned bullish, upgrading 2Q26 to 5.5% saar (from 3.3%) and the full year to 1.2%, citing the April beat, the May trade surplus and a World Cup consumption tailwind [JPM J094, 26 Jun 2026].
  • Citi stayed cautious, cutting its 2026 consumption forecast to 1.7% (from 2.6%) and investment to −1.7% (from +1%) on the "likely delay in USMCA negotiations into next year," and keeping GDP at 1.1% — pointing to consumer confidence at a post-2022 low (43.5) and softening formal employment [Citi C021, 18 Jun 2026; Citi C008, 11 Jun 2026].
Divergence · the 2Q bounce

By end-June JPM read the April data as the start of a real rebound (2Q +5.5% saar, FY 1.2%); Citi read it as fragile and demand-led-by-services-only, holding FY at 1.1% and trimming the demand components. Same data, opposite conviction — the growth-side mirror of the rates chapter, where JPM's view recovered faster.

The structural picture (barely changed all period)

  • Investment = the weak spot. Machinery & equipment contracted throughout (falling since 2024); construction was the only support and a volatile, public-spending-driven one that JPM suspected was "a fad" [JPM J037, 6 Apr 2026]. Hope rests on Plan Mexico (a MXN 5.6tn 2026–30 infrastructure plan) and a USMCA deal — both largely 2027 stories [Citi C011, 5 Feb 2026; JPM J058, 8 May 2026].
  • Consumption = resilient but fading. Supported by the 13% minimum-wage hike and the real wage bill, but dragged by deteriorating consumer confidence (44 → 43.5, lowest since 2022), soft remittances and slowing consumer credit [Citi C008, 11 Jun 2026; JPM J055, 6 May 2026].
  • Labour = tight but softening. Unemployment drifted 2.6% → 2.8% (still below the 3.4% 10-yr average), but formal job growth was flattered by digital-platform reclassification — ex-platform creation ran near zero [Citi C001, 16 Apr 2026; Citi C008, 11 Jun 2026].
  • The forecast outlier. The Finance Ministry's 2026 growth assumption of 2.3% towered over JPM (1.2%), Citi (1.1%), Banxico (1.1%) and consensus (~1.3%) — a gap that fed scepticism about the government's fiscal targets [JPM J061, 13 May 2026; JPM J077, 29 May 2026].

Theme 04 · External policy

Trade, USMCA & Tariffs

This was the single biggest overhang of the half-year — and, paradoxically, a story where Mexico kept coming out ahead on the actual numbers while the uncertainty did the damage. On tariffs, Mexico ended up a relative winner: most exports are USMCA-compliant, so successive US tariff waves left its effective rate around 3–4½%, low versus peers. But the USMCA review — legally due 1 July 2026 — slid into an open-ended renegotiation that, by both desks' reckoning, will not conclude in 2026, and that lingering question mark is what kept investment frozen all period.

Phase 0 — Mexico plays nice (Dec – Jan)

Mexico opened by aligning with Washington: it raised tariffs on 1,400+ non-FTA (mostly Asian) products and lifted Asian-vehicle duties from 20% to 50% — Economy Minister Ebrard's "produce here to sell here" — a move the US welcomed as positive for the 2026 review because it curbs Chinese transshipment [Citi C023, 18 Dec 2025]. A December Water-Treaty deal also defused a threatened 5% Trump tariff. The flip side: those same tariffs were a (modest) source of January's inflation worry (covered in the Inflation chapter).

Phase 1 — Tariff waves wash out in Mexico's favour (Feb)

A messy US tariff sequence ended up benign for Mexico. The US Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA "fentanyl" tariffs; Trump then announced a generalised 10%→15% import duty — but USMCA-compliant exports (~85% of the total) were exempt, making the net effect "arguably positive" [Citi C017, 27 Feb 2026]. JPM put Mexico's effective US tariff at "nearly 3%, from around 5% before" and called Mexico "one of the global winners" [JPM J022, 23 Feb 2026]; Citi's estimate was a bit higher at 4.4%. Section 232 tariffs (autos 25%, steel & aluminium 50%) stayed in place — the persistent exception.

Divergence · the effective tariff

JPM ~3% ("tariff winner") vs Citi ~4.4% ("positive but limited") — same IEEPA/Section-122 facts, more conservative Citi framing. Both agreed the direction was favourable and that some tariff would always remain.

Phase 2 — The review starts early, then stretches out (Mar – Apr)

Rather than wait for 1 July, Mexico and the US launched the review early (formal start 5 March; first Ebrard–Greer round mid-March), with Mexico the "lead channel" ahead of Canada [JPM J030, 20 Mar 2026; JPM J043, 17 Apr 2026]. The agenda: stricter rules of origin, transshipment, critical minerals, and energy (a recurring "bottleneck"). Ebrard's priority was rolling back the sectoral auto/steel/aluminium tariffs — but the US conditioned even a partial steel/aluminium cut (50%→25%) on strict US-content requirements, and Ebrard conceded the sectoral tariffs "won't disappear" with a deal [Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026]. By April, USTR Greer was openly saying the review "may extend beyond the July 1 deadline." JPM reframed its base case as a "renegotiation, not a revision."

Phase 3 — No deal by July; near-term termination ruled out (May – Jun)

Negotiating rounds were scheduled into Washington (mid-June) and back to Mexico (July), confirming the deadline would pass with the existing framework simply rolling on [Citi C009, 28 May 2026]. JPM called a 1-July rubber-stamp "far-fetched," and flagged the process could run to end-2027 or beyond 2028 [JPM J074, 29 May 2026]. The noise peaked in June: Mexico and Canada announced intent to extend the pact 16 years, while Trump said he was "not looking to renew" and would "rather not have" USMCA — read by Citi as a negotiating tactic that nonetheless raised the odds of the review spilling past 2026 [Citi C008, 11 Jun 2026]. The period closed with Ebrard clarifying the "worst case" is a 10-year extension, not cancellation — effectively ruling out near-term termination [Citi C022, 25 Jun 2026]. A new wrinkle: a US Section 301 forced-labour 10% tariff (effective 2 June), and reports Mexican autos could face a ~19% average tariff vs ~15% for Korea/Japan — a hint Mexico's tariff edge may be eroding [Citi C008, 11 Jun 2026].

The real economic cost: frozen investment

Both desks were emphatic that the damage ran not through the tariff level but through uncertainty. Capital-goods imports stayed weak all period ("structural hurdles to investment"), and Banxico's own business survey ranked rule-of-law and trade uncertainty as the top disincentives to relocating to Mexico [JPM J029, 13 Mar 2026; JPM J093, 26 Jun 2026]. Citi tied its June consumption/investment downgrades directly to the "likely delay in USMCA negotiations into next year" [Citi C021, 18 Jun 2026]. There were green shoots — GM said it would shift Chevrolet Aveo assembly to Coahuila from 2027 — but the investment revival is, on both desks' telling, a 2027 story contingent on a deal. JPM's late-June structural note also cautioned that 2025's export strength was partly a tech-price illusion: import growth was driven by Asian tech prices, not volumes, offering "limited support for growth" [JPM J075, 26 Jun 2026].

Theme 05 · External accounts

Balance of Payments, FX & External

The external accounts were, quietly, a source of strength — and the peso was the half-year's understated hero. USDMXN appreciated ~17% from the start of 2025, and both desks spent the period revising their year-end forecasts stronger, not weaker. The trade balance swung to surplus, the current-account deficit stayed small (~0.5–1.0% of GDP), and foreigners returned to Mexican government debt. The two soft spots: remittances (shrinking in peso terms and pressured by US immigration policy) and new FDI (frozen by the USMCA overhang).

The peso: a one-way appreciation

The dominant FX story was relentless peso strength, which both houses attributed mostly to generalised USD weakness plus an attractive carry. Citi's year-end-2026 USDMXN forecast was marked stronger again and again — from 19.4 (Dec) → 18.9 (Jan) → 18.0 (Feb) → ~17.6–17.9 (May–Jun) — while JPM ran even stronger at 17.30 eop [Citi C007, 19 Feb 2026; JPM J059, 8 May 2026]. On a marketing trip in February, investors floated the peso breaking below 17.00 [Citi C014, 11 Feb 2026]. The March oil shock dented it only ~2.6% intra-quarter, and it stayed stronger YTD throughout [JPM J030, 20 Mar 2026]. A strong peso was the connective tissue across themes — it anchored core-goods disinflation, but it also crimped peso oil revenue (Fiscal) and remittance purchasing power.

Trade balance: swung to surplus on a tech-led export boom

2025 closed with a small trade surplus (~US$0.8bn, from a US$18.5bn deficit in 2024), as a non-energy surplus of ~US$26bn offset a widening energy deficit [Citi C020, 30 Jan 2026]. Into 2026 the monthly path was choppy — a January import-surge deficit gave way to growing surpluses in March–May. Exports were powered by non-auto manufacturing tied to the US tech/AI boom and a metals-price surge in mining; autos lagged on tariffs [JPM J072, 25 May 2026]. By late June the YTD surplus was ~US$5.8bn and oil exports hit their highest since 2024 (price and volume both up) [JPM J093, 26 Jun 2026]. The caveat (per JPM's structural note): much of the tech trade is price-driven, so the growth dividend is smaller than the headline export numbers suggest.

Current account, FDI and portfolio flows

The current-account deficit stayed comfortably financed: just 0.4% of GDP in 2025, widening modestly to ~0.6% in 1Q26 (US$15.9bn that quarter) [Citi C017, 27 Feb 2026; JPM J073, 27 May 2026]. Beneath it:

  • FDI ~2.0% of GDP but stagnant — 1Q26 inflows were the lowest for a first quarter since 2022, with new investment weak and the USMCA overhang the cited culprit [JPM J073, 27 May 2026].
  • Portfolio flows turned friendlier — net inflows of ~0.4% of GDP, with foreigners piling into government debt at the fastest pace since 4Q23, even through the rating actions [JPM J073, 27 May 2026].
  • Reserves sat around US$250–280bn; Citi sees ~US$257bn by end-2026 [Citi C022, 25 Jun 2026].

Remittances: the soft spot

Remittances were the one external line under genuine pressure. After falling ~3.9% in 2025 (linked to US immigration/deportation policy), 2026 saw a tepid USD recovery (~+2–4% oya) but an outright decline in peso terms (−10% to −13% oya) as the strong currency eroded purchasing power [Citi C010, 8 May 2026; JPM J079, 1 Jun 2026]. The composition told the story: "fewer but larger deposits" — transaction counts falling while average transfers rose, which JPM linked to tougher US enforcement (ICE activity, new customer-ID/due-diligence rules) [JPM J079, 1 Jun 2026]. Remittances held at ~3.1–3.2% of GDP, still above the pre-pandemic ~2.9%.

Divergence · current-account & FX

JPM was the more constructive: a 0.5%-of-GDP 2026 CAD (on World Cup tourism, solid exports, a remittance rebound) and a stronger peso (17.30). Citi saw a wider 1.0%-of-GDP deficit (weaker remittances, recovering import demand) and a slightly softer peso (~17.9). Modest gap, same benign direction.

Theme 06 · Public finances

Fiscal & Sovereign Ratings

The fiscal story built steadily to the half-year's clearest negative event: a sovereign downgrade. Mexico entered 2026 having run a 4.8%-of-GDP deficit in 2025 — far above the 3.9% approved — dragged by weak oil revenue and ongoing Pemex support. A loosened fiscal rule, optimistic official growth assumptions and rigid social spending then convinced the agencies that consolidation was slipping: S&P moved to a negative outlook (12 May) and Moody's downgraded Mexico to Baa3 (20 May), leaving it one notch above high-yield at two of the three agencies. Both desks see the deficit stuck near 4.3–4.8% of GDP in 2026, with debt grinding toward ~55% and Pemex the recurring culprit.

Phase 0 — A wider-than-billed 2025 (Dec – Feb)

The headline shock came early: the full-year 2025 deficit landed at 4.8% of GDP versus an approved 3.9% (and Citi's own 4.5% call), on end-year spending normalisation, weak oil revenue and sovereign support to Pemex [Citi C011, 5 Feb 2026]. Public debt rose to ~52.6% of GDP. The Finance Ministry preferred to frame it as a 4.3% deficit excluding a Pemex financial operation — but the broadest measure (PSBR) was later pegged at 5.9% of GDP for 2025 in the 2027 pre-criterios [Citi C002, 9 Apr 2026]. Fitch and S&P were already warning that fiscal strength had "ceased to be a strength."

Phase 1 — The fiscal rule gets softer (Mar – Apr)

In March the executive moved to weaken the fiscal-responsibility rule by excluding social programs from the capped "structural expenditure" measure — easing compliance with a rule already breached in recent years (subsidies had risen from 1.7% of GDP in 2018 to ~2.8% by 2025) [Citi C012, 27 Mar 2026]. Citi's read: it "increases the odds of fiscal slippage… probably not enough to trigger a rating action" on its own. The 2027 pre-criterios then set PSBR targets of 4.1% (2026) and 3.7% (2027) — implying programmable-spending cuts both desks doubted, given a Finance-Ministry growth assumption (2.3%) far above everyone else's. Fitch reaffirmed BBB− (stable) on 16 April, comfortable with its headroom, even as it flagged consolidation risk [Citi C001, 16 Apr 2026].

Phase 2 — The downgrade cascade (May)

Three weeks in May delivered the event the period had been building toward:

  • S&P → negative outlook on its BBB rating (12 May), citing slow consolidation, faster-than-expected debt buildup, a higher interest burden and Pemex; S&P projected a 4.8%-of-GDP 2026 deficit [JPM J061, 13 May 2026].
  • Moody's → downgrade to Baa3 from Baa2 (stable outlook, 20 May) — front-loaded ahead of USMCA clarity, citing rigid spending, a narrow revenue base and continued Pemex support. JPM had pre-called it: "only a matter of time" [JPM J066, 21 May 2026].

Moody's was explicit on the mechanism: a ~1%-of-GDP federal primary adjustment in 2025 was halved by ~US$35bn (1.9% of GDP) of direct Pemex support, with another US$14bn (0.7%) budgeted for 2026 and "further support… expected absent a material improvement" [JPM J066, 21 May 2026]. The silver lining both desks floated: a downgrade could finally pressure the government into "full-fledged, credible fiscal consolidation."

Theme 07 · Institutions

Politics, Security & Institutions

President Sheinbaum stayed popular but increasingly cornered. Her approval held in the high-60s/low-70s — propped up by social programs — even as it slid ~16pp from its early-2025 peak, with security the soft underbelly. Three threads ran through the half-year: a security-policy pivot toward confronting cartels (welcomed by Washington), a US-indictment crisis that put ruling-party figures in the dock and forced her to choose between the US and her own party, and a steady erosion of institutional checks (judicial/electoral reforms) that both desks flagged as a drag on investment and the USMCA narrative.

The security pivot (Feb)

The turning point was the 22 February military killing of the CJNG cartel leader, which triggered clashes but signalled Sheinbaum "distancing herself from the complacent 'hugs, not bullets' approach" of her predecessors — and was expected to play well with the US ahead of the USMCA review [JPM J024, 27 Feb 2026; Citi C017, 27 Feb 2026]. Mexico also handed 37+ alleged cartel members to US authorities and arrested senior CJNG figures. Her approval actually rose to 72% in February on the back of it [Citi C004, 5 Mar 2026]. The backdrop was constant US pressure — Trump's Venezuela operation, an offer of military help (which she declined on sovereignty grounds), and warnings of US action on cartels.

The "Achilles' heel": US indictments of ruling-party figures (Apr – Jun)

The defining political crisis arrived in late April, when the US indicted prominent Morena politicians on cartel-conspiracy charges — Sinaloa Governor Rubén Rocha and Senator Enrique Inzunza ("the most prominent Mexican official charged in the US since 2020"), with governors of Sonora and Tamaulipas reportedly under investigation by June [Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026; Citi C018, 4 Jun 2026]. This put Sheinbaum "in a difficult position: confront the US or confront her own party." She refused to hand over a governor, hardened her anti-US rhetoric (raising alleged CIA activity and sovereignty violations), and leaned on the defence that 77% of the public already rated her crime-handling poorly — a vulnerability heading into the 6 June 2027 midterms (lower chamber + 17 governorships) [Citi C018, 4 Jun 2026].

Institutional erosion: electoral and judicial reform

The thread most relevant to markets was the steady weakening of autonomous institutions:

  • Sheinbaum's first legislative defeat — her constitutional electoral reform (cutting proportional-representation seats and party financing) was rejected on 11 March when coalition allies PT/PVEM withheld support, exposing a coalition fracture [JPM J027, 13 Mar 2026]. She fell back to a watered-down "Plan B," passed by the Senate 87-41.
  • The judicial/electoral package (late May) — postponed judicial elections from 2027 to 2028, introduced "foreign interference" as grounds to annul elections (a direct response to the US indictments), and — most controversially — let some pro-Morena electoral magistrates seek re-election to 2034. JPM called the foreign-interference clause "the most politically sensitive element" and the package "too little too late" for rule-of-law perceptions [JPM J074, 29 May 2026; Citi C018, 4 Jun 2026].

Both desks repeatedly linked this "erosion of autonomous entities" and weakened rule of law to depressed investment sentiment and added friction in the USMCA talks [JPM J043, 17 Apr 2026; JPM J058, 8 May 2026]. A partial counterweight: Sheinbaum's willingness to break with Morena's doctrinaire wing on growth (e.g. reopening the fracking debate, an investment-streamlining plan) suggested pragmatism where it served the economy [JPM J042, 17 Apr 2026]. Separately, a 40-hour workweek law (phased 2027–2030) passed — a coalition priority that business groups warned on for labour costs.

The political bind in one line

Social programs (subsidies up from 1.7% to ~2.9% of GDP since 2018) keep approval high but worsen the fiscal trap; security failures and US indictments erode that approval; and the institutional reforms used to lock in power are exactly what deter the investment Mexico needs. Politics, fiscal and growth are knotted together.

Theme 08 · Energy

Pemex & Energy

Energy was where Mexico's fiscal, inflation and growth stories converged. Pemex remained the fiscal millstone the rating agencies fixated on — soaking up ~US$35bn in 2025 support against a depleted production base. The March oil shock tested the system, but the government's gasoline excise-tax cap absorbed the blow at the consumer level (at a fiscal cost). And in the period's most notable policy shift, the Sheinbaum administration quietly reopened the fracking debate — reframing unconventional gas as "energy sovereignty."

Pemex: the fiscal millstone

Mexico's oil production has fallen from ~2.5 mbd to under 1.7 mbd, turning the country into a net energy importer with an energy trade deficit of ~US$25.6bn in 2025 (widening to ~US$27bn on the oil shock) [Citi C004, 5 Mar 2026; Citi C009, 28 May 2026]. Propping up Pemex required a large, multi-channel rescue in 2025 — a MXN 254bn transfer, a US$11.3bn sovereign-guaranteed "P-cap" structure, and a MXN 250bn vehicle to clear contractor debt [Citi C011, 5 Feb 2026]. Moody's quantified it bluntly: ~US$35bn (1.9% of GDP) of direct support in 2025, US$14bn (0.7%) budgeted for 2026, and more expected absent operational improvement — the explicit trigger for its downgrade [JPM J066, 21 May 2026]. Pemex's own ratings sit deep in high-yield (Moody's B1, Fitch BB+).

The oil shock and the gasoline cap (Mar – May)

The Middle East conflict pushed Citi's oil scenarios to a base of Brent ~$80–90 (de-escalating to ~$62) and a bull case near $120; JPM ran the Mexican mix at ~$90/bbl [Citi C004, 5 Mar 2026; JPM J031, 20 Mar 2026]. The shock fed inflation only at the margin (airfares, electricity) because the government used its excise-tax mechanism to keep regular gasoline under Sheinbaum's MXN 24/litre cap — applying a 24% excise discount from late March, the first cut since the full tax took effect in March 2025 — and capped diesel at MXN 27, with Pemex absorbing part of the cost [Citi C012, 27 Mar 2026; Citi C015, 30 Apr 2026]. The cap worked for consumers but came at a fiscal price: forgone excise revenue, made worse because the strong peso muted the offsetting oil-revenue windfall. By late May the caps were straining supply — gasoline shortages appeared in several states as controlled prices lagged import costs [Citi C009, 28 May 2026].

The policy pivot: "energy sovereignty" and fracking (Apr – May)

The period's structural surprise was a softening of Mexico's long-standing anti-fracking stance. Pemex's 2025–35 plan embedded 113bn boe of prospective resources (57% unconventional), and the administration began using language like "complex geology" rather than "fracking" — which JPM judged "more than semantics," reflecting a pivot toward reducing dependence on US gas imports (Mexico consumes ~9.1 Bcf/d but produces only ~2.3) [JPM J045, 23 Apr 2026]. The framing — energy sovereignty — lets fracking double as security-of-supply, industrial policy and an upstream-investment story. JPM was careful to temper the upside, citing "execution risk, water-use concerns, regulatory credibility, social opposition, and Pemex's financial and operating limitations." On power, an investment plan targets +22,000 MW via mixed public-private capital, but the legally-mandated 46% cap on private participation in electricity remains [JPM J058, 8 May 2026].

A late glimmer (Jun)

The period closed on a modestly better note for the oil balance: May oil exports hit their highest since 2024, with Pemex data showing both higher prices and higher export volumes [JPM J093, 26 Jun 2026] — though fiscal oil inflows still lagged, held back by the strong peso and a deliberate policy of limiting exports to support domestic self-sufficiency.

Why energy matters across the digest

Pemex support is the agencies' core downgrade rationale (Fiscal); the gasoline cap is what kept the oil shock out of headline inflation (Inflation) — at a fiscal cost amplified by the strong peso (BoP); and the fracking pivot is part of the same pragmatic, growth-over-ideology turn seen in the politics chapter.
